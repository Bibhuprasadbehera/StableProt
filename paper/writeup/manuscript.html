Abstract

Accurate prediction of protein thermostability (Tm) and organismal optimal growth temperature (OGT) is essential for enzyme engineering, biocatalyst design, and metagenomic bioprospecting. Yet experimental Tm assays, despite offering high-fidelity thermodynamic endpoints, are low-throughput and slow, leaving Tm data scarce. To address this gap, we present StableProt, a structure-aware deep learning framework that couples 3Di conformational token embeddings (SaProt) with disjoint per-task pathways for Tm and OGT, and that returns a calibrated predictive interval rather than a point estimate. To limit data contamination, we enforce a bidirectional homology audit (<30% sequence identity) between training and every evaluation benchmark. Combined with mesophilic downsampling, this lets StableProt generalize from a curated dataset of under 1 million records.

Every baseline compared here emits a bare point estimate; StableProt emits a predictive distribution. Scored by the continuous ranked probability score (CRPS), which for a point forecast equals its mean absolute error so that no baseline is penalized for its output format, StableProt attains 4.52 °C on the empirical ProThermDB benchmark against 5.76 °C for the strongest baseline, and 8.71 °C on the zero-shot FireProtDB out-of-distribution holdout against 12.76 °C. On point accuracy StableProt reaches 6.16 °C MAE on ProThermDB, where TemBERTure remains lower at 5.76 °C, and 11.85 °C on FireProtDB, the lowest of any model tested. A single global variance scale fitted out-of-fold reduces expected calibration error from 14.4% to 1.9%, after which 67.5% of observations fall inside the nominal 68.3% interval. On OGT, bin-balanced scoring shows StableProt is the only model that does not degrade outside the mesophilic band, mitigating the systematic under-prediction of thermophiles that affects existing predictors above 40 °C. StableProt is available as a web server at [URL].

Keywords: Tm prediction, thermostability, protein language model, uncertainty quantification, calibration, optimal growth temperature, enzyme engineering, biocatalyst

1. Introduction

Temperature is a fundamental determinant of the conformational dynamics, folding kinetics, and thermodynamic stability of biological macromolecules. Consequently, the identification of enzymes capable of sustaining catalytic activity under elevated thermal conditions represents a central objective in industrial biotechnology, diagnostic assay engineering, and synthetic biology.1 Historically, the characterization of protein thermal stability has relied on experimental biophysical assays such as differential scanning fluorimetry (DSF), circular dichroism (CD), and differential scanning calorimetry (DSC). While these techniques yield high-fidelity empirical melting temperatures (Tm), they require purified protein samples and labor-intensive protocols, rendering them intractable at the scale of the hundreds of millions of uncharacterized sequences generated by modern metagenomic sequencing.2,3 To circumvent these experimental bottlenecks, computational approaches have increasingly leveraged machine learning to predict protein thermostability directly from primary sequence data. Early statistical methods applied linear regression and support vector machines to classical physicochemical features, including aliphatic indices, dipeptide composition, and hydrogen-bonding networks.4,5

The advent of self-supervised protein language models (pLMs), including ProtTrans6 (ProtT5-XL), the ESM-2 family7, and SaProt, transformed sequence modeling by capturing rich evolutionary conservation and structural dependencies within high-dimensional token representations. Building on these embeddings, specialized predictors such as TemStaPro, TemBERTure8, DeepSTABp9, ESMStabP10, and ThermoFormer11 have achieved notable success in inferring thermal properties from sequence alone.

Despite these methodological advances, existing computational tools exhibit four related limitations that compromise their reliability in real-world biological deployment. The first, and the one that motivates most of what follows, is a proxy domain shift that manifests as systematic under-prediction of thermostable proteins. Owing to the scarcity of experimental Tm records, fewer than 35,000 curated entries globally, many approaches instead rely on OGT datasets derived from host organism metadata, as in PRIME and ThermoFormer, or are pretrained on OGT before being fine-tuned on the comparatively limited Tm corpus. The underlying distributions of both Tm and OGT are heavily skewed toward the 30–50 °C mesophilic range, and consequently predictors with poor fidelity in extremophilic regimes can nonetheless attain favorable MAE scores, since the overwhelming majority of sequences cluster within this narrow, well-represented band. Adding to this problem, specialized models typically predict either OGT or Tm in isolation12, with none learning a joint representation of both, yet forcing a single network to predict both organismal growth limits and individual protein unfolding thresholds introduces a physical domain shift, as mesophilic proteins frequently possess Tm values well above their host's environmental growth limit.13 When a model fails to disentangle these two distributions, it undergoes what we refer to throughout as mesophilic collapse: the systematic under-prediction of thermophiles, with values clustered near 40–50 °C even when the model is confronted with hyperthermophilic variants that unfold above 80 °C.

The second limitation is one of benchmark hygiene, and it determines whether any reported improvement can be believed. Uncontrolled sequence overlap between training and benchmark sets inflates reported metrics, and under strict sequence separation at less than 30% identity, models reliant on nearest-neighbour memorization display marked accuracy loss and fail to generalize to novel engineered enzymes. We make no claim about the composition of other groups' training partitions, which we cannot inspect, and report only the separation we enforced on our own splits. The third arises inside multi-task architectures: where a shared backbone serves simultaneous Tm and OGT prediction, conflicting gradient updates from disparate loss landscapes degrade the representational capacity of the dedicated unfolding head, so the auxiliary task purchases its benefit at the expense of the task that matters. The fourth is a question of data. High-fidelity empirical Tm measurements remain limited relative to macro-environmental OGT annotations, which are themselves noisy and imprecise proxies for protein-specific thermodynamic stability, so the abundant signal and the relevant signal are not the same signal.

To address these challenges we developed StableProt, shown in Figure 1, which replaces the traditional shared regression head with disjoint neural pathways operating on structure-aware 3Di alphabet representations from SaProt. Heterogeneous auxiliary features are routed through dedicated projection heads and optimized via alternating backpropagation, and the two pathways are trained without overlap between the training and evaluation splits. StableProt also returns a predictive interval alongside each estimate, calibrated so that the stated coverage matches the observed coverage; no other predictor evaluated here reports one, which is why the comparison in §3 is conducted under a proper scoring rule that treats a point prediction as a degenerate distribution rather than penalizing it for its format. Table 1 sets out what each compared method predicts, from what input, and in what output format, so that those comparisons can be read without ambiguity.

2.2 Data curation and mesophilic subsampling

Experimental protein melting temperatures were curated from three independent sources: ProThermDB, Meltome Atlas, and the TemBERTure curated records. Because empirical unfolding records exhibit protocol-dependent variation (pH, ionic strength, heating rate), deduplication and outlier removal were applied. Records sharing identical UniProt accessions or exact amino acid sequences were grouped, and groups whose internal Tm spread exceeded 10.0 °C were discarded as experimental outliers. For surviving groups the consensus unfolding temperature is the sample median, giving one label per unique polypeptide sequence. We additionally applied a biophysical consistency filter that removed 2,148 records in which the reported Tm fell below the host organism's optimal growth temperature. Figure S1 shows the label distributions before and after each of these steps.

OGT annotations were curated from BacDive and integrated proteomic manifests, with sequences diverging by more than 20.0 °C from host taxonomy baselines removed. The final training corpus comprised 940,000 curated OGT records and a holdout validation split of 47,000 sequences. Sequences were restricted to lengths between 50 and 2,000 amino acids, and non-standard residue symbols (X, B, Z, J) were filtered.

Mesophilic subsampling. In the source OGT database, 42.3% of records sit at exactly 28, 30 or 37 °C, and 87% lie between 25 and 40 °C. Retaining a randomly sampled 14% of mesophilic sequences (25.0–40.0 °C) per epoch, while retaining all psychrophilic (<25.0 °C) and thermophilic (>40.0 °C) sequences, shifts thermophile representation from 16.8% to 38.0% (ogt_subsample_meso_rate = 0.14; Figure 1A inset, Figure S1). Because roughly 84% of database OGT annotations are rounded integers, which produce step-function plateaus during regression, continuous target jitter (σ = 0.5 °C) is applied during forward passes. OGT targets are z-score normalized during training to prevent raw temperature magnitudes from dominating the loss gradient. All hyperparameters named in this section and the next three are collected with their rationale in Table S1.

2.3 Bidirectional homology separation

To limit performance inflation, we separated training and evaluation partitions by homology in both directions. MMseqs2 was run at a 30% sequence identity threshold (e-value < 10−3) for the Tm splits, and CD-HIT at 40% identity for the OGT splits (CDHIT_IDENTITY = 0.40, chosen because CD-HIT v4.8.1 is algorithmically unstable below 40%). We cross-referenced the ProThermDB validation split and the independent FireProtDB out-of-distribution benchmark against both training partitions, and removed any training record sharing at least 30% identity with any evaluation protein before embeddings were generated. The verification step reported in Figure 1A is an exact-sequence check; the homology criterion is the clustering threshold stated above, and the operative thresholds differ between the two tasks.

2.4 Structure-aware 3Di embeddings

Protein structure influences thermal stability through salt bridges, hydrophobic core packing and secondary-structure rigidity, so we used the frozen SaProt (650M) structure-aware encoder to generate 1,280-dimensional representations. SaProt operates on a folded alphabet that concatenates each amino acid token with a structural conformational state derived from Foldseek, as shown in the lower half of Figure 1A.

For the Tm corpus, tertiary structures were predicted with ESMFold (facebook/esmfold_v1). Sequences longer than 1,400 residues were folded with OpenFold, and sequences exceeding 800 residues that triggered out-of-memory conditions were processed by progressive chunking and truncation. Predicted coordinates were converted to 3Di tokens with Foldseek and matched 1:1 with the primary sequence; where folding was capped, the tokens cover the folded region 1:1 and the remaining tail residues are padded with the mask token (#). For the 940,000-sequence OGT corpus, full structure prediction was computationally intractable on the available hardware, so the OGT pathway uses sequence-only representations. The OGT head is therefore not structure-aware, which we return to in the Limitations.

2.5 Disjoint multi-head architecture

StableProt uses completely independent multi-layer perceptron pathways rather than shared hidden layers (Figure 1B). Each pathway accepts the 1,280-dimensional SaProt embedding together with task-specific auxiliary scalars: 9 for Tm (predicted OGT prior, transmembrane flag, sequence length, and 6 amino acid composition ratios) and 8 for OGT (the same features without the OGT prior). The composition ratios are the charged fraction (D+E+K+R)/L, hydrophobic fraction (A+I+L+M+F+W+V)/L, proline fraction P/L, cysteine fraction C/L, aromatic fraction (F+W+Y)/L, and the aliphatic index of Equation (1):

\[ \mathrm{AI} \;=\; \min\!\left( \frac{A + 2.9\,V + 3.9\,(I+L)}{100\,L},\; 1.0 \right) \](1)
\mathrm{AI} \;=\; \min\!\left( \frac{A + 2.9\,V + 3.9\,(I+L)}{100\,L},\; 1.0 \right)

To prevent the 1,280-dimensional embedding from numerically overwhelming the low-dimensional biological scalars, the auxiliary features are first projected through dedicated bottlenecks, Linear(9, 64) and Linear(8, 64), each followed by layer normalization and a GELU activation, Equation (2):

\[ h_{\text{aux}} \;=\; \mathrm{GELU}\big( \mathrm{LayerNorm}( W_{\text{proj}}\, x_{\text{aux}} + b_{\text{proj}} ) \big) \;\in\; \mathbb{R}^{64}, \qquad x_{\text{hidden}} \;=\; \big[\, e_{\text{SaProt}} \,\big\Vert\, h_{\text{aux}} \,\big] \;\in\; \mathbb{R}^{1344} \](2)
h_{\text{aux}} \;=\; \mathrm{GELU}\big( \mathrm{LayerNorm}( W_{\text{proj}}\, x_{\text{aux}} + b_{\text{proj}} ) \big) \;\in\; \mathbb{R}^{64}, \qquad x_{\text{hidden}} \;=\; \big[\, e_{\text{SaProt}} \,\big\Vert\, h_{\text{aux}} \,\big] \;\in\; \mathbb{R}^{1344}

Each 1,344-dimensional vector feeds an independent three-layer residual block (1344 → 512 → 256 → output), with dropout of 0.3 and 0.2 applied independently per pathway.

What is and is not shared. The two pathways share no trainable parameters: aux_proj, fc1, ln1, fc2, ln2, res and head are all duplicated per task, and the SaProt encoder is frozen. Information passes in one direction only, at the level of predictions: the OGT estimate enters the Tm pathway as an auxiliary feature. We therefore describe the design as a shared frozen representation with task-specific pathways and a directed OGT-to-Tm link, rather than as multi-task learning, since the two objectives are never optimized jointly through shared weights. Supplementary Note 1 sets out why this distinction matters for interpreting the gradient analysis in §3.5.

2.6 Loss formulations

Tm pathway. The output layer predicts a mean \(\mu\) and a raw variance parameter \(v\). Variance is bounded below to prevent collapse and numerical underflow, and the pathway is optimized by the Gaussian negative log-likelihood, Equation (3):

\[ \sigma^2 \;=\; \mathrm{Softplus}(v) + 10^{-4}, \qquad \mathcal{L}_{T_m} \;=\; \frac{1}{2}\left[\, \log \sigma^2 \;+\; \frac{(y-\mu)^2}{\sigma^2} \,\right] \](3)
\sigma^2 \;=\; \mathrm{Softplus}(v) + 10^{-4}, \qquad \mathcal{L}_{T_m} \;=\; \frac{1}{2}\left[\, \log \sigma^2 \;+\; \frac{(y-\mu)^2}{\sigma^2} \,\right]

To account for experimental disagreement across the literature, samples are weighted by the inverse of their interquartile range, with the median IQR of multi-reading proteins imputed for single-reading proteins, Equation (4):

\[ w_i \;=\; \frac{1}{1 + \left( \mathrm{IQR}_i / 6.34 \right)^2}, \qquad \mathrm{IQR}_i \leftarrow 0.62\ ^\circ\mathrm{C} \ \text{ if single reading} \](4)
w_i \;=\; \frac{1}{1 + \left( \mathrm{IQR}_i / 6.34 \right)^2}, \qquad \mathrm{IQR}_i \leftarrow 0.62\ ^\circ\mathrm{C} \ \text{ if single reading}

so that proteins with high experimental consensus receive more gradient weight. A Huber component (huber_delta_tm = 5.0) bounds the influence of extreme residuals.

OGT pathway. The OGT head predicts a mean and a variance. The mean is trained by a focal Huber loss that concentrates gradient on rare extremophilic outliers, with \(\delta = 15.0\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\), \(\gamma = 2.0\) and \(\beta = 0.5\) in z-score space, modulated by inverse bin-frequency reweighting with the multiplier clamped at 22.0. The variance is trained by Gaussian NLL computed against a detached mean, so that the variance gradient cannot reshape the mean:

\[ \mathcal{L}_{OGT} \;=\; \mathcal{L}_{\text{focal-Huber}}(\mu, y) \;+\; \lambda \cdot \mathrm{NLL}\big( \mathrm{sg}[\mu],\ \sigma^2,\ y \big) \](5)
\mathcal{L}_{OGT} \;=\; \mathcal{L}_{\text{focal-Huber}}(\mu, y) \;+\; \lambda \cdot \mathrm{NLL}\big( \mathrm{sg}[\mu],\ \sigma^2,\ y \big)

where \(\mathrm{sg}[\cdot]\) denotes the stop-gradient operator in Equation (5), and the bin reweighting multiplier of Equation (6) is

\[ m_b \;=\; \mathrm{clip}\!\left[ \left( \frac{\mathrm{median}_{b'} n_{b'}}{n_b} \right)^{0.75},\ 0.3,\ 22.0 \right] \](6)
m_b \;=\; \mathrm{clip}\!\left[ \left( \frac{\mathrm{median}_{b'} n_{b'}}{n_b} \right)^{0.75},\ 0.3,\ 22.0 \right]

The detached-mean term is what makes the OGT interval usable: a variance head trained jointly with the mean degenerates into a function of the mean, whereas the detached formulation leaves point accuracy unchanged while producing a variance that is correctly scaled without post-hoc inflation (§3.3, Figure S2C). Replacing the point loss entirely with NLL was tested and degraded OGT MAE from 13.6 to 21.4 °C, so the hybrid is retained. Supplementary Note 2 records this and the four other interventions that were tested and rejected.

2.7 Optimization and two-stage inference

The two pathways are maintained as disjoint modules optimized by independent AdamW instances (learning rate 1×10−4, weight decay 1×10−5) with CosineAnnealingWarmRestarts (T0 = 10, Tmult = 2) and early stopping after 15 epochs without improvement. Training uses automatic mixed precision, and embedding augmentation noise (σ = 0.02, applied with probability 0.15) is added during forward passes.

Two-stage inference. At inference the embedding first passes through the OGT head to produce an environmental prior \(\hat{T}_{OGT}\), which is then supplied to the Tm pathway (Figure 1B, lower panel). During training the Tm head receives the ground-truth OGT with Gaussian jitter of σ = 2.0 °C, which regularizes the head against over-reliance on an exact prior. Predictions are aggregated across a five-seed ensemble, and the predictive variance combines the aleatoric and epistemic components by the law of total variance, Equation (7):

\[ \sigma^2_{\text{pred}} \;=\; \underbrace{\frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S} \sigma_s^2}_{\text{aleatoric}} \;+\; \underbrace{\frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S}\big(\mu_s - \bar{\mu}\big)^2}_{\text{epistemic}} \](7)
\sigma^2_{\text{pred}} \;=\; \underbrace{\frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S} \sigma_s^2}_{\text{aleatoric}} \;+\; \underbrace{\frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S}\big(\mu_s - \bar{\mu}\big)^2}_{\text{epistemic}}

rather than by the standard error of the ensemble mean, which understates the width of a predictive interval by roughly a factor of two.

2.8 Evaluation metrics

We report mean absolute error (MAE), root mean squared error, Pearson r, Spearman ρ, Matthews correlation coefficient, F1, ROC AUC for thermostability classification at Tm ≥ 60 °C, and top-10% enrichment precision. Both count-weighted (micro) and bin-balanced (macro) averages are given wherever a per-temperature breakdown is available, since the two answer different questions on a distribution this skewed.

Continuous ranked probability score. Because StableProt returns a predictive distribution while every baseline returns a single value, the two output formats must be scored on one scale. CRPS is the integrated squared difference between the predicted cumulative distribution \(F\) and the step function at the observation, Equation (8), and for a Gaussian forecast it has the closed form of Equation (9):

\[ \mathrm{CRPS}(F, y) \;=\; \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \big[\, F(x) - \mathbb{1}\{x \ge y\} \,\big]^2 \, dx \](8)
\mathrm{CRPS}(F, y) \;=\; \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \big[\, F(x) - \mathbb{1}\{x \ge y\} \,\big]^2 \, dx
\[ \mathrm{CRPS}\big(\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^2),\, y\big) \;=\; \sigma\left[\, z\big(2\Phi(z)-1\big) \;+\; 2\phi(z) \;-\; \frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}} \,\right], \qquad z \;=\; \frac{y-\mu}{\sigma} \](9)
\mathrm{CRPS}\big(\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^2),\, y\big) \;=\; \sigma\left[\, z\big(2\Phi(z)-1\big) \;+\; 2\phi(z) \;-\; \frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}} \,\right], \qquad z \;=\; \frac{y-\mu}{\sigma}

where \(\Phi\) and \(\phi\) are the standard normal distribution and density functions. Two properties matter here. As \(\sigma \to 0\) the expression tends to \(|y-\mu|\), so a deterministic forecast scored by CRPS receives exactly its own mean absolute error: baselines are scored on the output they publish, with nothing imputed and no penalty added. And CRPS is a proper scoring rule, minimized only by a forecast distribution matching the data-generating distribution, so widening an interval cannot improve it. Both properties are what the interval-adjusted error of Equation (10) lacks, which is why CRPS carries the model comparison in this work.

Interval-adjusted error (Int-MAE). When the observation falls inside the predicted band the error is taken as zero; otherwise it is measured from the band boundary, Equation (10):

\[ \text{Int-MAE}_k \;=\; \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \max\!\big( 0,\; |y_i - \hat{y}_i| - k\,\sigma_i \big) \](10)
\text{Int-MAE}_k \;=\; \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \max\!\big( 0,\; |y_i - \hat{y}_i| - k\,\sigma_i \big)

This quantity is interpretable, since at \(k=1\) it is the mean distance by which the model lands outside its own stated one-sigma band, but it is not a proper scoring rule and it decreases monotonically as \(\sigma\) grows. It is therefore reported only at an explicitly stated \(k\), in the calibration subsection, and never as a cross-model comparison.

Calibration. Rather than assuming a fixed multiplier, we fit a single global variance scale \(c\) on held-out data by two-fold cross-fitting, so that no observation contributes to the scale applied to it, Equation (11):

\[ c^{\star} \;=\; \arg\min_{c>0} \ \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \mathrm{CRPS}\big(\mathcal{N}(\mu_i,\, c^2\sigma_i^2),\, y_i\big), \qquad \text{interval} \;=\; \mu_i \pm 1.96\,c^{\star}\sigma_i \](11)
c^{\star} \;=\; \arg\min_{c>0} \ \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \mathrm{CRPS}\big(\mathcal{N}(\mu_i,\, c^2\sigma_i^2),\, y_i\big), \qquad \text{interval} \;=\; \mu_i \pm 1.96\,c^{\star}\sigma_i

We stress that this is a predictive interval for a single future measurement, not a confidence interval on a parameter. Expected calibration error is the mean absolute gap between empirical and nominal coverage across a grid of nominal levels, Equation (12):

\[ \mathrm{ECE} \;=\; \frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M} \left| \ \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \mathbb{1}\Big\{ |y_i - \mu_i| \le z_m\, c\, \sigma_i \Big\} \;-\; p_m \ \right| \](12)
\mathrm{ECE} \;=\; \frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M} \left| \ \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \mathbb{1}\Big\{ |y_i - \mu_i| \le z_m\, c\, \sigma_i \Big\} \;-\; p_m \ \right|

On this in-distribution benchmark TemBERTure is ahead on point error and on linear correlation, and both differences are larger than sampling error: MAE 5.76 against 6.16 °C (95% CI on the paired difference [+0.20, +0.58]) and Pearson r 0.826 against 0.788 ([+0.029, +0.049]). On rank correlation the two are indistinguishable, at ρ 0.516 against 0.517 ([−0.026, +0.025]), so the ordering StableProt induces over proteins is as good as TemBERTure's even where its absolute values are worse. ThermoFormer-TM attains the highest correlations of any model tested on this benchmark (r = 0.829, ρ = 0.563), ahead of StableProt on both ([+0.031, +0.051] and [+0.021, +0.070]), while carrying 0.89 °C more point error. StableProt has the lower error against DeepSTABp, ESMStabP and ThermoFormer-TM, and the lowest CRPS of any model, but in-distribution point accuracy is not where its advantage lies.

The deficit in linear correlation reflects predictions that are over-dispersed relative to the measured values, which is the same property that produces the lower error out of distribution: less regression toward the mesophilic mean. It cannot be repaired after the fact. Pearson r is invariant to affine rescaling and Spearman ρ to any monotone transform, and isotonic regression, quantile mapping and affine correction were each tested and left r unchanged or degraded both r and the MAE.

Where the interval pays. Scored by CRPS, which reduces to mean absolute error for a point forecast and therefore scores each baseline on its own published output, StableProt attains 4.52 °C against TemBERTure's 5.76 °C (paired difference −1.24 °C, 95% CI [−1.40, −1.08]; Table 2, and Figure 2C for the cross-benchmark view). The margin over every other model is wider still, from −2.53 °C against ThermoFormer-TM to −4.62 °C against ESMStabP, and no interval approaches zero. The advantage comes from the model reporting a calibrated interval where the baselines report a single number, and the interval that earns it is characterised in §3.4.

Temperature-wise breakdown

Performance across 10 °C intervals is uneven for every model (Figure 2B). StableProt attains lower error than TemBERTure in three of the six populated bins, covering 61.2% of the benchmark (50–60, 80–90 and 90–100 °C), is level with it in the 60–70 °C bin (10.79 against 10.72 °C), and attains the lowest error of any predictor tested in the 90–100 °C bin (3.31 °C, n = 13). TemBERTure nonetheless attains the lower overall error under both count-weighted (5.76 against 6.16 °C) and bin-balanced averaging (6.45 against 6.63 °C), and that difference is produced entirely by two bins: 40–50 °C contributes +0.35 °C and 70–80 °C contributes +0.33 °C to the aggregate gap of +0.39 °C, and together they hold 23.6% of the data. The other four bins contribute −0.29 °C between them. We do not claim the best performance above 80 °C: pooled over the two highest bins StableProt reaches 4.36 against TemBERTure's 5.81 °C, but ThermoFormer-TM is lower still at 3.56 °C and ESMStabP at 3.90 °C, both because they win the 80–90 °C bin.

3.2 Zero-shot generalization on FireProtDB

To evaluate generalization in the engineering setting, where candidate enzymes share little sequence similarity with known databases, we benchmarked all models on the FireProtDB holdout at <30% identity against all training data (n = 322; lower block of Table 2, per-protein scatter in Figure S3A).

Under these constraints StableProt attains the lowest point error of any model tested (11.85 °C, against TemBERTure 12.76, DeepSTABp 13.59, ThermoFormer-TM 13.61 and ESMStabP 14.91 °C). The margin over each baseline excludes zero under a paired bootstrap: against TemBERTure, 95% CI [−1.64, −0.20]; against the other three, upper bounds of −1.01 or lower. StableProt also attains the highest rank correlation on this benchmark (ρ = 0.350 against 0.214 for TemBERTure; [+0.021, +0.252]), and the gap is significant against ThermoFormer-TM as well ([+0.008, +0.227]). Under CRPS the margin widens to 8.71 against 12.76 °C (95% CI [−4.74, −3.32]). Bin-balanced, the separation is wider than the aggregate suggests: 15.53 °C against 16.79 for TemBERTure and 19.41 for ESMStabP, and StableProt attains the lowest error in every bin above 60 °C, which holds 40% of this benchmark.

The ordering is the reverse of the in-distribution result in §3.1. Taken together the two benchmarks describe a model that gives up some accuracy on densely sampled mesophilic proteins in exchange for not collapsing when the target is unfamiliar, which is the regime the method is intended for. Figure S3B shows the distributional form of this: baseline errors skew negative on the thermostable tail, while StableProt's stay centred.

3.3 Environmental adaptation profiling (OGT)

We evaluated the OGT pathway on an external out-of-distribution benchmark (BRENDA, n = 525) and an internal BacDive test split (n = 4,854), against the two dedicated OGT predictors, PRIME and ThermoFormer (Table 3, Figure 3).

The count-weighted comparison is unfavourable and we report it first. On BRENDA, StableProt's point error is 10.84 °C against 6.48 for ThermoFormer and 6.75 for PRIME; on BacDive it is 7.94 against 4.71 and 4.96 °C. On the most commonly reported metric the dedicated OGT models are clearly better.

The bin-balanced comparison inverts. Both baselines attain 2–3 °C error in the 20–40 °C band and 11–13 °C in the 40–60 °C band (Figure 3B), a near four-fold degradation that coincides exactly with the transition from data-dense to data-sparse temperature regimes. StableProt's profile is close to flat across the range, and Figure 3C reduces this to a single ratio: dividing each model's 40–60 °C error by its 20–40 °C error gives 0.76 for StableProt against 3.69 for PRIME and 3.78 for ThermoFormer. Weighting each 10 °C bin equally, StableProt attains the lowest error of the three on the internal split (9.23 against 9.81 for ThermoFormer and 10.38 for PRIME), and under CRPS it is ahead on both splits (Table 3). We note that all three models draw OGT labels from overlapping public sources, so a substantial part of the count-weighted comparison reflects how completely each model has absorbed a shared annotation table, rather than protein-level inference.

3.4 Uncertainty calibration

Raw predictive variances are under-dispersed by a factor of approximately 1.6. A single global variance scale, fitted by two-fold cross-fitting so that no observation contributes to the scale applied to it, reduces the expected calibration error on ProThermDB from 14.4% to 1.9% (c = 1.65; Figure 4A, and Figure S2A for the full range of nominal levels). After scaling, 67.5% of observations fall inside the nominal 68.3% interval and 91.4% inside the nominal 95.4% interval, at mean half-widths of 7.4 and 14.8 °C respectively. On the out-of-distribution FireProtDB holdout the same procedure gives 67.4% and 90.4% coverage at half-widths of 14.4 and 29.0 °C, but requires a larger scale (c = 3.62), reducing calibration error from 31.6% to 2.8%. Coverage and sharpness are reported together throughout, in Table 1 and again here, because coverage on its own can be bought with width; Table S4 collects the full set with the interval-adjusted error alongside.

The calibration is marginal, not conditional, and the distinction matters for how the interval should be used. Stratified by measured Tm, coverage of the nominal 68.3% interval on ProThermDB is 76.5% between 40 and 60 °C (n = 2,409), 31.7% between 60 and 80 °C (n = 693) and 81.1% above 80 °C (n = 238); on FireProtDB it is 92.5% between 40 and 60 °C (n = 159), 59.6% between 60 and 80 °C (n = 104) and 8.2% above 80 °C (n = 49). The 60 to 80 °C band is the failure case on both benchmarks, and it is the band that matters most for thermostable enzyme discovery. Figure 4B shows the same result as reliability curves per regime: the aggregate figure in Figure 4A averages over strata that are miscalibrated in opposite directions.

Consistent with this, the predicted interval width is close to constant across the benchmark, its coefficient of variation being 0.22, and the rank correlation between predicted σ and realised absolute error is 0.04 on ProThermDB and 0.02 on FireProtDB. Mean absolute error by σ quintile is 5.92, 5.96, 6.55, 6.13 and 6.22 °C: flat, and not monotone (Figure 4C). A single constant width fitted to each benchmark scores marginally better under CRPS than the learned per-protein width (4.49 against 4.52 °C on ProThermDB, 8.64 against 8.71 on FireProtDB), which is the sharpest statement of the same finding. The interval should therefore be read as a calibrated population-level width and not as a per-protein reliability score. What it provides is an honest statement of typical uncertainty, which is what the CRPS comparison measures, rather than a means of triaging individual candidates.

3.5 Architecture ablations

Retaining only 14% of mesophilic samples per epoch shifts thermophile training representation from 16.8% to 38.0% and reduces hyperthermophilic error above 80 °C from 9.83 to 8.00 °C (Figure 4D, left).

Tracking gradient inner products across training shows that when a shared hidden backbone optimizes Tm under Gaussian negative log-likelihood and OGT under focal Huber loss simultaneously, the two gradients are negatively correlated (mean cosine similarity −0.045 over 150 sampled steps, negative at every one of them; Figure 4D), and the shared-backbone control carries a 1.50 °C performance penalty relative to the disjoint design (Figure 4D, centre; Table S2). The disjoint pathways share no parameters, so their gradient cosine similarity is identically zero by construction rather than as an empirical finding, and we present it as such.

The apparent tension between this result and the OGT-to-Tm link resolves because the two concern different things. Gradient interference is about sharing parameters; the prior shares information, one-way, at the level of predictions. That the information is worth sharing can be measured directly: supplying the Tm head with the true OGT rather than the predicted one lowers validation error from 6.27 to 3.61 °C, and a constant prior gives 5.00 °C (Table S2b). The premise holds; the limiting factor is the accuracy of the predicted prior on Tm proteins, not the value of the prior itself. Supplementary Note 1 develops this distinction; the constant prior was tested and rejected on architectural grounds, as recorded in Supplementary Note 2.

3.6 External benchmarks: Meltome, family clusters and single mutations

On the FLIP Meltome / Megascale holdout (781 proteins) StableProt attains MAE 7.85 °C (r = 0.44, ρ = 0.24; Figure 5A). Error falls with target temperature rather than rising: 16.02 °C below 40 °C (n = 27), 7.79 °C between 40 and 60 °C (n = 660) and 5.91 °C above 60 °C (n = 94), the opposite of the mesophilic overfitting pattern.

Under cluster-based family separation with MMseqs2 (--min-seq-id 0.3 -c 0.8) the evaluation corpus partitions into 5,861 homology clusters, across which the overall error is 6.35 °C (Figure 5C); the largest clusters give 5.02 °C (n = 18, mean Tm 62.4 °C), 6.62 °C (n = 16, 60.1 °C) and 6.19 °C (n = 16, 60.0 °C). Figure S3C plots error against cluster size across all 5,861 clusters; the trend is flat, indicating the model is not simply interpolating within large, well-populated families. Within individual organism proteomes the model retains positive rank correlation (Figure 5D), indicating discrimination beyond species-level amino acid composition. Linear probes on the frozen embeddings also recover signal on tasks the model was never trained for (Figure 5B); these are reported as an observation about the representation and carry no claim of state of the art.

On the FireProtDB single-point mutation set (n = 3,649 experimentally measured ΔTm values) StableProt attains a ΔTm error of 5.05 °C MAE with weak but non-random rank agreement (ρ = 0.21, r = 0.19) and 56.8% directional accuracy (ROC AUC = 0.585; Figure S3D). The model was not trained on mutational data, and the typical single-mutation effect is smaller than its predicted interval, so we do not present it as a ΔTm predictor. What the result shows is that the interval behaves as intended: it widens to cover an effect the model cannot resolve rather than reporting false precision. The design tab of the web server is accordingly positioned as a hypothesis generator for loop-region targeting, not as a ΔΔG predictor.

4. Discussion

StableProt separates Tm and OGT into disjoint pathways that share no parameters, so the two objectives cannot compete for capacity, and reports a calibrated interval with each prediction. On the out-of-distribution holdout it attains the lowest point error of any predictor tested, and on both Tm benchmarks it is ahead of every predictor under CRPS (Table 2); in distribution TemBERTure remains the more accurate point predictor.

Data efficiency. StableProt is trained on fewer than 1 million records. Mesophilic subsampling shifts thermophile representation in training from 16.8% to 38.0%, and the model attains the lowest point error and the lowest CRPS of any predictor tested on the zero-shot FireProtDB holdout. We do not claim that larger corpora are unnecessary in general; the comparison here is confined to Tm prediction, and models trained at greater scale may support tasks not evaluated in this work.

What the per-bin OGT analysis shows. PRIME and ThermoFormer achieve excellent performance in the 20–40 °C range, where training data is abundant, but their error increases four- to five-fold in the 40–60 °C range (Figure 3B, reduced to one ratio per model in Figure 3C). This is not a flaw peculiar to those models; it reflects the underlying data distribution, since OGT databases are heavily biased toward mesophilic organisms and a model trained on such data will interpolate well in the dense region and generalize poorly outside it. StableProt's profile is nearly uniform across bins, which is why it is behind on count-weighted error and ahead on bin-balanced error (Table 3). For bioprospecting, where thermophilic enzymes are the targets, the second is the more relevant summary, and we report both rather than choosing one.

Why we score with CRPS. Every predictor in this comparison returns a single number with no stated uncertainty. An experimentalist deciding whether to synthesize a candidate needs to know the width of the plausible range, and a model fitted by maximum likelihood already carries that information. We therefore score all methods with CRPS, a proper scoring rule under which a deterministic forecast receives exactly its own mean absolute error; no method is penalized for the output format it publishes, and because the rule is proper, widening an interval cannot improve the score. Under this scoring the interval is worth 1.24 °C in distribution (4.52 against 5.76 °C, 95% CI [−1.40, −1.08]) and 4.04 °C out of distribution (8.71 against 12.76 °C, 95% CI [−4.74, −3.32]). We also report the mean error falling outside the predicted 95.4% band, 0.43 °C on ProThermDB, which is directly interpretable but is not a proper scoring rule and so does not carry the comparison. This is a field-level observation rather than a criticism of any one method: every published predictor evaluated here discards uncertainty information the model already computes, and that information is what experimentalists need.

Benchmark hygiene. Benchmark hygiene materially affects reported accuracy in this area. We separated training and evaluation sets by MMseqs2 at <30% sequence identity for Tm and CD-HIT at 40% for OGT, and we removed 2,148 records whose reported Tm fell below the host organism's growth temperature. We make no claim about the composition of other groups' training partitions, which we cannot inspect. Separately, the checkpoint control in §3.1 quantifies a related hazard: an OGT predictor scored against Tm labels reaches r = 0.778 while sitting roughly 15 °C from the truth, so rank agreement between a proxy and a target says little about whether the proxy can be used in place of it.

Limitations

The interval is a width, not a ranking. Two limitations bound the uncertainty claim. First, the predicted variance is calibrated marginally but not conditionally: it is close to the correct average width, yet its rank correlation with realised absolute error is 0.04 on ProThermDB and 0.02 on FireProtDB, and a single constant width fitted to each benchmark scores marginally better under CRPS (4.49 against 4.52 °C, and 8.64 against 8.71 °C). Diagnostically the variance head learned to track the predicted mean (rank correlation 0.38) rather than the error, and because the model under-predicts thermophiles, the interval widens for proteins predicted to be thermostable rather than for those whose predictions are actually poor (Figure 4C). Retraining the variance component against a detached mean corrected the scale of the interval but not its informativeness, which indicates that the two properties are separable and that the second is the harder one. Users should treat the interval as a population-level width and not as a triage signal for individual candidates. Second, the variance scale does not transfer across benchmarks: the factor required is 1.65 on ProThermDB and 3.62 on the FireProtDB holdout (Table 1 and Table S4), so a scale fitted in distribution understates the uncertainty out of distribution, which is exactly where it matters most. Conditional coverage degrades in the same direction: above 80 °C the nominal 68% interval covers 81% of ProThermDB observations but only 8% of FireProtDB observations (Figure 4B).

A systematic gap at 70–80 °C. The model performs worst in class in this bin (10.94 °C against TemBERTure's 4.94 °C; Figure 2B), and the cause is bias rather than data scarcity: the bin holds 3.79% of training labels, more than the 80–90 °C bin (3.61%) where the model does well. Within the bin the model predicts a mean of 86.0 °C against a truth mean of 75.2 °C and over-predicts 97% of the cases. Mean predictions across bins run 53.4, 55.4, 61.3, 86.0, 87.3 and 88.4 °C, so the model jumps from roughly 61 to roughly 86 °C with little in between, and places only 6.6% of its predictions in the 65–82 °C window where 10.0% of the measured values lie. The gap persists under every OGT prior we tested, so it is a property of the Tm head rather than of the prior.

Other limitations. Psychrophiles are the weakest regime, with raw errors of 29.10 and 21.14 °C in the 0–10 and 10–20 °C OGT bins. The Tm pipeline depends on ESMFold, which dominates inference latency and constrains throughput. The OGT corpus has no structures, so the OGT head is not structure-aware despite the framework being described as such for Tm. Performance degrades measurably when host organism OGT metadata is unavailable, as documented in §3.7. And TemBERTure retains an in-distribution accuracy advantage that this work does not overturn.

Looking forward, StableProt provides an open-source, reproducible suite for guiding directed evolution, stabilizing industrial enzymes, and annotating metagenomic sequence space. Extensions incorporating molecular dynamics flexibility could improve predictions for cold-adapted variants, and a variance head that is informative per protein, rather than merely correctly scaled, remains the clearest open problem raised by this work.
